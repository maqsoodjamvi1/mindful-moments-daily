When my therapist first told me to take a deep breath to calm my racing mind, I tried it immediately. I sat up straight, inhaled deeply through my nose until my chest expanded, and held it for a few seconds. Within ten seconds, my heart rate spiked, my throat felt tight, and a wave of pure panic washed over me.

If you have had this exact experience, you probably felt like something was broken inside you. If mindfulness and deep breathing are supposed to relax humans, why did doing it make you feel like you were suffocating or having a heart attack?

As it turns out, you are far from alone, and your body is not malfunctioning. In fact, your nervous system is doing its job a little too well. Here is why deep breathing can backfire when you are anxious, and how I finally learned to use breathwork without triggering a panic response.

Why “Take a Deep Breath” Can Trigger a Panic Response

To understand why deep breathing can make anxiety worse, we have to look at how our bodies react to perceived threats. When you are chronically stressed or anxious, your nervous system gets stuck in a low-grade fight-or-flight mode. Your breathing naturally becomes shallow, fast, and chest-based.

When you suddenly force yourself to take a massive, exaggerated breath, a few physiological things happen that can trick your brain into thinking you are in danger:

  • Over-oxygenation and CO2 shifts: Taking huge gulps of air rapidly changes the balance of oxygen and carbon dioxide in your blood. This can cause lightheadedness, tingling in the fingers, and dizziness—physical sensations that mimic the onset of a panic attack.
  • Hyper-vigilance: When you have anxiety, you are constantly scanning your body for danger. Sitting in a quiet room and forcing yourself to focus entirely on your breath turns up the volume on every physical sensation. A normal flutter in your chest suddenly feels like a cardiac event.
  • The control trap: Forcing a specific breathing pattern can feel like a chore or a test you are failing. If you miss a beat or feel like your lungs aren’t filling up enough, frustration sets in, which releases more adrenaline.

For years, I thought I just wasn’t “zen” enough to meditate. The truth was that standard breathing advice—like filling the lungs to maximum capacity—was entirely wrong for my specific nervous system state.

Shift From Big Breaths to Slow Breaths

Once I realized that big, dramatic inhales were the enemy, I had to completely change my approach. The secret to calming anxiety through breathwork isn’t taking bigger breaths; it is taking slower, softer breaths.

Instead of trying to fill your lungs completely, focus entirely on the exhale. The exhale is where the magic happens for your nervous system. When you make your exhale longer than your inhale, you stimulate the vagus nerve, which tells your brain that it is safe to downshift out of fight-or-flight mode.

Try this simple adjustment the next time you feel anxious:

  1. Inhale gently through your nose for a count of just three seconds. Let it be a small, quiet breath—barely audible.
  2. Exhale slowly through your mouth (like you are blowing through a tiny straw) for a count of six seconds.
  3. Repeat this for just one minute. Do not worry about expanding your belly or perfecting your posture. Just focus on making that outgoing breath twice as long as the incoming one.

By shrinking the inhale, you avoid the lightheadedness and the physical shock to your system. By lengthening the exhale, you manually activate your body’s natural brakes.

Ditch the Rules and Find Your Anchor

Another mistake I made early on was trying to force myself into rigid breathing frameworks like Box Breathing or the 4-7-8 method when I was already panicked. When your anxiety is at an eight out of ten, trying to remember a complex numerical sequence just creates more mental friction.

If counting breaths makes you tense, drop the numbers entirely. Instead, use physical touch as an anchor while you breathe. Put one hand flat on your desk or press your feet firmly into the floor. As you breathe out, focus entirely on the sensation of gravity holding you down.

Breathwork should never feel like a performance or a test you need to pass. If a specific technique makes your chest feel tight, stop doing it immediately. There is no single “correct” way to breathe your way to calm, and finding what actually works for your unique body takes a bit of trial and error.
